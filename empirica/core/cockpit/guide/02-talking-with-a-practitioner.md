# 2. Talking with a practitioner

## Work with it, not just through it

You get the best results when you treat the practitioner as a capable colleague who has
just joined your team: very fast, very knowledgeable, but new to *your* situation.

- **Say why, not only what.** "Draft a reply to Anna; she is upset about the delay and we
  want to keep her as a client" gives far better results than "reply to Anna".
- **Say what done looks like.** "One paragraph, friendly, offers a call this week."
- **Share what you already know.** If you tried something that did not work, say so. It
  saves the practitioner from repeating it.

## Think out loud together

You do not need a finished request. It is fine to say "I am not sure what we should do
about the pricing page, what do you think?" The practitioner can lay out options, say which
it would pick and why, and ask you the one question that decides it.

## Disagree freely

If an answer looks wrong, say so and say why. A good practitioner holds its position when it
has evidence, and changes its mind when you bring new facts. It should not simply agree
with you to be pleasant. If it changes its answer only because you pushed, ask it what
changed.

## Keep requests to one subject at a time

Mixing three unrelated asks in one message makes each of them weaker. If you have several,
list them; the practitioner can turn them into a plan (page 5).

## When it reports back

A practitioner reports in a fixed shape: what is **done** (with proof, such as a link, a document
or a sent email), what is **next**, and what is **waiting on you**. Read the last part first:
those are the decisions only you can make.
