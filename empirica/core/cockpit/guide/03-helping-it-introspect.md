# 3. Helping it think about its own thinking

A practitioner can be confidently wrong, the same way a person can. The good news: it is
good at examining its own reasoning when you ask. A few short questions often turn a shaky
answer into a solid one.

## Questions that work

| Ask | What it does |
|---|---|
| "How do you know that?" | separates what it checked from what it assumed |
| "What are you assuming here?" | brings hidden guesses into the open |
| "What would change your mind?" | shows how firm the answer really is |
| "How sure are you, and why?" | gets an honest confidence, with its reason |
| "What don't you know yet?" | lists the gaps before they cause trouble |
| "What could go wrong if we do this?" | surfaces risks you had not mentioned |

## Checked, read, or guessed

A practitioner can know something in three ways, and they are not equally strong:

1. **It checked**: it tried it and saw the result. Strongest.
2. **It read**: it opened the document, the email or the web page. Strong.
3. **It guessed** from what usually happens. Useful, but treat it as a guess.

When something matters, ask which of the three it is. If it is a guess, ask it to check.

## Share your own thinking too

Your hunches are useful data. "My feeling is the client cares more about speed than price"
gives the practitioner something to test. It may confirm it, or show you something that
does not fit. Either way you both learn more than if you had kept it to yourself.

## It writes down what it learns

When a practitioner discovers something, makes a decision, or notices it got something
wrong, it writes it in the practice's notebook. You may see this happen. It is how the next
session starts smarter than this one.
