# 4. Using its predictions

## Ask for the answer it expects

A practitioner is a strong predictor: given what it has read, it can usually tell you what
the right choice probably is. Use that. Instead of asking you an open question, a good
practitioner brings you its **predicted answer** and the one reason behind it:

> "Should we send the invoice today? I recommend yes: the work was accepted yesterday and
> the contract says invoice on acceptance. OK?"

You can then answer with one word, "yes", or override it: "no, wait for Monday". You never
have to work out the options yourself.

## When it does not give you one

If a practitioner asks you an open question ("What should we do?"), ask it back:
"What do you predict, and why?" If it cannot predict, it usually means it has not looked at
the material yet. Ask it to look first.

## Predictions you can check later

A practitioner can also make predictions about the future, "if we change the headline,
more people will sign up", and the practice can record them. Later you can check whether it
was right. Over time this shows how good its predictions are in this area, and where to
trust it more or less.

## A prediction is not a decision

The practitioner predicts; **you decide** anything that involves money, people, contracts,
or something hard to undo. A recommendation marked "(Recommended)" is its best guess, not a
choice it has made for you.
